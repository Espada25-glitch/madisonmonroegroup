When execution gets cheaper, judgment matters more

John C. Gale · Founder & Principal, Madison & Monroe

A business can produce more marketing and still be uncertain about where to grow.

Patrick Coffee’s recent Wall Street Journal report describes small companies giving AI agents substantial marketing responsibilities, including campaign development and lead generation. The people overseeing those systems still spend time reviewing their work and making decisions about the business.

For owners and executives, that raises a practical question: as execution becomes easier to access, where should leadership attention go?

My view is that it should move toward the choices that give execution its purpose.

Consider a service business evaluating a new market. AI can help assemble research, draft outreach, and develop campaign variations. Those capabilities may make an initial test faster and less expensive. The owner still has to determine whether the market fits the firm’s capabilities, whether serving it would strain existing commitments, and what evidence would justify further investment.

Three decisions deserve particular attention.

Which customers are worth pursuing? A larger audience is not automatically a better opportunity. Customer fit depends on the problem you solve, the economics of serving that customer, and the relationships you can sustain. More leads help only if they move the business toward its objectives.

What promise can the business deliver? Marketing can create expectations faster than operations can adapt. Before expanding outreach, leaders need to test whether their people, service model, and capacity can support the offer. A compelling message needs a credible business behind it.

Where should resources go—and what should wait? Lower production costs can make more initiatives look attractive. Time, attention, and trust remain limited. A useful strategy sets priorities, makes trade-offs explicit, and defines what would cause the business to change course.

AI can contribute to each of these decisions. It can organize evidence, challenge an assumption, and suggest alternatives. The responsibility for choosing, committing resources, and responding to the consequences remains with leadership.

For a small business, a useful starting point is one defined question. Choose an opportunity to investigate, establish the evidence needed, and agree on a review point before expanding the effort. Measure whether the work improves a commercial decision, alongside how much time it saves.

More execution can be automated. Your next move still needs judgment.
