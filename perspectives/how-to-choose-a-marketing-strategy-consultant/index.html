How to choose a marketing strategy consultant

John C. Gale · Founder & Principal, Madison & Monroe

A useful marketing strategy consultant helps you make better choices about customers, positioning, and investment. Before comparing firms, define the decision you need to make and the evidence you will need to act on the recommendation.

For an owner-led business, the question might be why referrals are strong but a new audience is not responding. A marketing leader might need to decide which segments deserve more investment. Another company may have a compelling offer but an unclear route to market. Those are different assignments. They should produce different scopes of work.

Start with the decision

Write the question in one sentence. For example: “Which customer segment should we prioritize for our next service launch?” That gives a prospective advisor a basis for explaining the work, identifying missing evidence, and defining a useful result.

Ask what decisions the engagement will support, who needs to participate, and which constraints must shape the recommendation. A strong plan should reflect the business’s capacity to deliver as well as its ambition to grow.

Look for a clear path from evidence to recommendations

Ask how the consultant will use customer conversations, sales information, market research, or channel performance to compare alternatives.

The answer need not be the same for every project. Existing evidence may be enough for a focused assessment; another decision may require additional research. What matters is that the advisor distinguishes what is known from what is assumed—and explains what would change the recommendation.

Make the deliverables concrete

“Strategic recommendations” can mean almost anything. Agree on the outputs you need. These might include a segmentation brief, positioning framework, go-to-market plan, ranked investment priorities, or a roadmap with owners and milestones.

Ask how the team will use each deliverable. A positioning framework should inform messages and customer conversations. An investment plan should help choose which initiatives to fund, test, or defer. A roadmap should identify dependencies and measures of progress.

Verify relevant experience

Look for experience that helps with your particular decision, and understand the context behind any result. An advisor’s work in a prior employer is different from a result delivered for a consulting client. Published research demonstrates a contribution to a field; it does not establish what every engagement will achieve.

In our Harvard Business Review article, “How to Get AI to Surface Your Brand,” Luca Cian, Luc Wathieu, and I examine how AI systems surface retail brands. One practical implication for a services business is to make its expertise understandable and its claims verifiable. That is useful when a buyer evaluates a consultant, too. Applying the findings to professional services remains something to test.

Clarify the working relationship

Establish who leads the work, who will conduct research, how specialists will be involved, and how decisions will be reviewed. Clarify whether the scope covers strategy alone or includes execution support. Agree on timing, fees, responsibilities, and the process for changing scope before work begins.

The right fit is a consultant whose approach matches the question, whose evidence can be assessed, and whose recommendations your team can use.
